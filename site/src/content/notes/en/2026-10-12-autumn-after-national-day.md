Autumn never seems to begin until the National Day break is over. From primary school through high school, the National Day holiday in early October was usually the longest break a Chinese student got apart from Spring Festival. But unlike Spring Festival, with its rounds of visits to relatives and other things no child had the slightest interest in, this was a holiday we could spend on nothing but play. Under the banner of celebrating the motherland’s birthday, I would spend whole days with the computer, our time together no longer limited to brief weekend dates. By then, though, the weight of a northern autumn was already quietly making itself felt—in thicker clothes, the turning leaves, and the chill of the morning breeze. Strangely, it was only after the holiday had ended that I would notice any of this. And every year, it was in class after class on that first day back that autumn’s bleakness really sank in. As though they had all agreed beforehand, every teacher would use that first lesson after the holiday to remind us of the same cruel fact: “Now that National Day is over, there are no more holidays this term until Spring Festival, so it’s time for everyone to settle down.” Year after year, those words held true with the certainty of a law of physics, and year after year, the news caught me completely off guard. Each time, another, unrelated thought would go off in my head, as punctual as an alarm clock: So autumn is here.

Why should autumn be bleak? For me, bleakness is a first derivative: not how bad things are, but the fact that they keep getting worse. In the months ahead, there would be no holidays, but there would be midterm exams. The weather would keep getting colder, the clothes would keep getting thicker, and the smog of those years would return along with them. As for the one bright prospect—winter break—whether it would be a happy one still depended on the mercy of whoever marked the final exams. On that day each year, I would find myself looking out of the window. The leaves on the plane trees, which had only just begun to yellow before the holiday, had already fallen away like sand through an hourglass. The few that remained swayed within that fixed frame, reminding me that they, too, would disappear. Did I care? I hated plane trees, but they were all that was left of the view from my window. After high school, life gradually changed, and I no longer had to wait for a holiday to do the things I liked. But the child I was could not have known that many years later, amid the bustle of Wuhan’s breakfast stalls, in the autumn drizzle of Aachen, beneath Michigan’s red leaves that tried to tell you summer had not yet burned itself out, beside the sprinklers that wet my feet on my way home from work in the Bay Area, I was to remember that distant afternoon after National Day, when I sat in class staring out of the window.
