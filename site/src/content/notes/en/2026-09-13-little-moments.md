> "Not everyone will accomplish something great. Some of us may just have to find meaning in the little moments of life." — Howard Wolowitz

My regret comes from this. There is nothing I truly love that I have held to consistently from childhood until now, nothing that would let me say out loud to other people, with real confidence, that I do understand this. As a "small-town swot" who grew up in a small county town in China, our lives really were planned out for us from the start, meaning the step up from primary school to junior high, the senior high entrance exam, the college entrance exam, finding a job, and working hard for promotions and money. I do not regret the effort I put in, because through my own effort I have reached a height that many people never reach (though of course this height is still one seen from inside the main quest of the swot). But whenever I look back in my free hours, I find that among all those "excellent qualities" that Chinese children are brought up to have, one alone was missing, which is knowing what you truly love. I envy the many friends who trained seriously at badminton with a coach from an early age. I envy the friends who took up an instrument as children and have kept it up until now. I envy the friends whose grades look worse than mine and whose salaries are not as high as mine, but who know, deep down, what kind of person they want to become. I have also started thinking about how I will raise my own children. I have been climbing a ladder the whole way. Along the way I have had to kick a lot of people off it, and along the way I have also been recognised by a lot of people. I have been given the title of top student, I have received commendations, and I crossed the ocean to one of the richest countries in the world. But once I stepped outside the circle where you can only feel happy by comparing yourself with other people, I found that inside I was barren. From childhood until now, all the exams, the papers published, the promotions and the rankings have been like a game. This is not what I truly love, and yet it is what I believed in and held to the whole way, the route on which a sense of self-approval could only come from comparing myself with other people, and for a time it was even the only route I had. After graduating I started reading like mad, learning to play an instrument and to improvise, and looking for other things I might truly love. I also began, almost as an act of revenge, to fade out of that restless circle I had been in. But there was a voice that used to go round and round in my head. You have held on this far, so you should spend your time on things that are genuinely "worth something". For instance, preparing for interviews outside work hours, so that you keep up with the age of "AI". You have already put in the better part of half a lifetime, so a little more will not hurt. A voice like that drove me through one late night after another and one exam after another, and it also made me give up, again and again, the direction my heart was restless to go in. Each time it was as though only "a little more" was needed. But was it really only a little more? Looking back now, I find that it is a divergent sequence, with no end to it. I hope my children will genuinely know what they like, and then hold to it. What they love will let them give off confidence and let them know what their real goals are, instead of forever doing the things that "look right". Their sense of their own worth will not collapse the instant the comparing disappears. May they always find meaning in the little moments of life.
